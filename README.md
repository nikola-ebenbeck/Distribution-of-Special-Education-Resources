# Distribution-of-Special-Education-Resources
Repositorium of the Study: "How could special educational resources be redistributed into inclusive primary education after the closing of special schools in urban and rural areas? – A simulation study"

**Abstract:**
Although inclusive education is a human right, parallel systems of special and regular schools still exist in major
parts of Germany. An inclusive education system necessitates the closure of special schools, followed by the
redistribution of special education personnel across all schools.
Simulation studies offer the opportunity to predict, based on data, the potential impacts of inclusive restructuring.
In this study, we utilize simulations to model the redistribution of personnel resources following the closure of
special schools. The simulations are based on primary school statistics from the German state of Bavaria, which
still maintains parallel special and regular school systems, as well as open geographical datasets. We explore how
the closing of all special schools (Model 1) or the closing of only special schools focusing on learning disabilities,
behavioural difficulties and speech, language and communication difficulties (Model 2) would affect the personnel
resources available to primary schools in different scenarios. Specifically, we consider how many special educators
would be available for an inclusive school system at primary level and how their distribution across schools of
varying sizes in urban and rural regions can ensure the permanent integration of special education teachers into
every primary school.

The results show that the closure of all special schools would result in 63.8% of primary schools receiving at least
one permanent special education teacher. On average, primary schools would be allocated 1.88 full-time special
education teacher positions (Min = 0.05; Max = 13.83). This proportion is reduced to 39.44%, in the case of the
closing of only some special school types. Large cities would receive significantly more resources than rural areas.
In rural regions, the number of students with special needs at a primary school often does not suffice to fill a fulltime
special education teacher position, necessitating the rotation of mobile special education teachers between
schools. This distribution could be managed in rural areas through geographical proximity and needs-based
allocation within districts, with one special education teacher assigned to two primary schools.
We discuss the necessity for parallel school systems to undergo comprehensive restructuring, particularly with
regard to resource allocation, in order to develop inclusive structures. Additionally, we highlight the need for the
permanent employment of special education teachers in inclusive schools and advocate for changes in both general
and special education teacher training to support the successful implementation of inclusive education.
